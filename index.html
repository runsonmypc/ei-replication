Frontier language models outscore human norms on emotional intelligence tests: a replication with newer models

Alejandro Legarda, a.legarda.j@gmail.com

Terms used on this page
STEM
Situational Test of Emotion Management: 44 short situations, each with four possible actions. The keyed answer, the one the scoring key marks as correct, is the action most experts chose.
STEU
Situational Test of Emotion Understanding: 42 situations. According to the original study, the keyed answer is the emotion or cause that appraisal theory predicts.
GEMOK-Blends
Geneva Emotion Knowledge Test, blends subtest: 20 short stories in which a person feels two emotions, described through their thoughts, feelings, urges, expressions and bodily signs. The keyed answer is the pair of emotion words whose signs the test's authors wrote into the story.
Proportion correct
Share of a test's items answered with the keyed option.
Run
One new conversation in which a model received the whole test and answered every item.
Effort
How much a model reasons before answering. At default effort the request sets no effort level, so each model uses its provider's default. Where documented, this is medium for most models and high for Claude Sonnet 5.5 and, on DeepSeek's own service, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. At maximum effort (max in tables and charts) the request asks for the provider's highest level.
Original six models
ChatGPT-4, ChatGPT-o1, Gemini 1.5 flash, Copilot 365, Claude 3.5 Haiku and DeepSeek V3, tested by the original study in December 2024 and January 2025.
Human norm
The mean score of the human sample used when each test was first published. For the STEU this was 200 psychology undergraduates in Australia, and for the STEM 112 of them. For GEMOK-Blends it was 180 English-speaking workers on the online work platform Amazon Mechanical Turk.
Reliable
Statistically significant at 0.05, two-sided, after correction for the number of comparisons tested together.
Scenario
An item's text without its answer options.
Model judge
A language model asked to rate how similar two scenarios are, on the 1 to 7 scale the original study's human raters used.
Flagged
Said of a new scenario whose highest rated similarity to any original scenario is above 5 of 7. The original study counted possible paraphrases by this rule.
Realism and clarity
The two questions the original study's participants answered after each item, on a 0 to 100 slider: how plausible or realistic, and how clear, the situation and its answer options are.
Model rater
A frontier model asked to rate how realistic and how clear an item is, on the original study's 0 to 100 sliders.
Panel
The frontier models taking a set of items as a test. The panel disputes an item's key when, counting each model equally, it preferred another option to the key.

This replication of Schlegel, Sommer and Mortillaro (2025, Communications Psychology) tests ten models released since that study, called the frontier models below: Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6.1 Terra, GPT-6 Luna, Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 3.8 Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. Each took the full Situational Test of Emotion Management (STEM), the full Situational Test of Emotion Understanding (STEU) and the blends subtest of the Geneva Emotion Knowledge Test (GEMOK-Blends), 10 times at its default reasoning effort and 10 times at maximum effort. Their scores are compared with the published human norms and with the six models the original study tested. Each frontier model also wrote new STEM and STEU items, as ChatGPT-4 did in the original study, and two of the frontier models rated how closely those items resemble the originals. The nine models that did not write an item set then rated its realism and clarity and answered it as a test, using checks first tested against the original study's participant data.

Findings

  • Every frontier model scored above the human norm on all three tests at both effort levels. Each scored at least 3.6 human standard deviations above the norm on the STEM, at least 1.4 on the STEU and at least 1.2 on GEMOK-Blends. The lowest STEU score, Claude Haiku 5.5's at default effort, is at about the 92nd percentile of the human sample. Original vs frontier models
  • At maximum effort the STEU is at its ceiling. Of the 10 frontier models, 8 answered all 42 items correctly in all 10 runs at maximum effort. GPT-6 Luna and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash were the exceptions. At default effort only GPT-6 Astra and Gemini 4 Argon had all 10 runs perfect. Original vs frontier models
  • GEMOK-Blends is close to its ceiling, and most of its remaining errors are on two items. In 8 of the 20 model and effort combinations, every run was perfect. Of the 141 wrong answers in 200 runs, 107 were on items 12 and 5, which the original six models also mostly answered wrongly. On both items, the frontier models' most common wrong answer was also the most common wrong answer of the people in the original study. GEMOK-Blends items
  • The frontier models scored reliably higher than the original six models on all three tests at both effort levels, although on the STEM at default effort the difference is reliable only with Gemini 3.8 Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash included. Counting each model's mean once, the frontier models were ahead by 0.142 proportion correct (p = 0.003) at default and 0.199 (p < 0.001) at maximum effort on the STEU, by 0.037 (p = 0.044) and 0.050 (p = 0.011) on the STEM, and by 0.104 (p < 0.001) and 0.119 (p < 0.001) on GEMOK-Blends. Original vs frontier models
  • Maximum effort reliably raised STEU scores for 3 frontier models, and no model's STEM score changed reliably. Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Terra gained +0.088 to +0.214 proportion correct on the STEU. Averaged over the ten models, maximum effort raised scores by +0.013 on the STEM (p = 0.047) and +0.056 on the STEU (p = 0.047). These averages are reliable only with Gemini 3.8 Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash included. Without them, p = 0.094 for each. On GEMOK-Blends the average change was +0.016 (p = 0.156), and only Claude Sonnet 5.5's score changed reliably, from 0.900 to 1.000. Output tokens per STEM run were 1.2 to 550 times as many at maximum effort as at default. Effort
  • Every frontier model rated scenario similarity closer to the mean human rating than the median human rater did. On the scenario pairs that the original study's human raters scored, the ratings of the best model, GPT-6 Astra, correlated 0.75 with the mean human rating, and those of the weakest, Claude Haiku 5.5, correlated 0.60. The median human rater's ratings correlated 0.52 with the mean of the other raters. Given how much the human raters disagree, the highest correlation a rater can be expected to reach is about 0.83. Judges
  • The frontier models' new STEU items were flagged as possible paraphrases far more often than ChatGPT-4's, but their new STEM items were not. On the STEU, the two model judges flagged 119 of 190 new items, against 3 of ChatGPT-4's 19 (p < 0.001). Of the 119, 97 were concrete versions of abstract originals (STEU items that describe a situation without a named person), and 5 were near-verbatim copies of original items shown as examples in the item-writing prompt. On the STEM, they flagged 34 of 180, against 5 of ChatGPT-4's 18 (p = 0.359). Paraphrase check
  • A panel of frontier models, first tested against the original study's participants, disputed the keys of fewer of the frontier models' new STEU items than of ChatGPT-4's. It disputed 10 of 190 new STEU keys (0 to 2 per model), against 4 of ChatGPT-4's 19 (p = 0.027). The model raters passed their test against participants for realism on both tests and for clarity on the STEM. Their STEU clarity ratings failed, and the STEM key check could not be tested, so neither was used. The raters scored the new STEU items slightly less realistic than ChatGPT-4's (−2.2 points of 100) and the new STEM items more realistic (+6.3) and clearer (+3.3). The STEM realism difference may overstate what participants would see, because the raters, unlike participants, rated ChatGPT-4's STEM items less realistic than the originals. Item quality

Item difficulty compared with humans

The original study's item-level data cover short forms of the STEM (18 items, 187 people) and the STEU (19 items, 185 people). Across these 37 items, the share of the original six models' answers that were correct correlated 0.71 with the share of people who answered correctly. For the frontier models the correlation was 0.61 at default and 0.51 at maximum effort. At maximum effort, 27 of the 37 items were answered correctly in every frontier run, so the correlation rests on the other 10 items, where it was 0.41. These data do not show whether the frontier models and people find different items hard.

Model judges against human similarity ratings

The paraphrase check below needs a rater of how similar two scenarios are. A scenario is an item's text without its answer options. To test language models as judges, each of the ten frontier models rated the 1,590 scenario pairs that human raters scored in the original study. These pair each of ChatGPT-4's 37 new scenarios with every original scenario of the same test (18 × 44 STEM and 19 × 42 STEU pairs). GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5, which come from different developers, were chosen as the two judges for the new items. The pair's averaged rating correlates 0.75 with the mean human rating and flagged 6 of the 7 ChatGPT-4 scenarios that the human raters flagged, plus 2 that they did not.

Paraphrase check of the model-written items

Each frontier model wrote 18 new STEM and 19 new STEU items with the original study's item-writing procedure (see Method). The two judges rated every new scenario against every original scenario of the same test, 15,900 pairs in all. A new scenario is flagged when its highest rated similarity to any original is above 5 of 7, the original study's rule for a possible paraphrase.

Each flagged scenario was put in the first of these four categories that fits its most similar original. Near-verbatim copy: the text matches that original almost word for word (a character-level match of at least 75%). Concrete version of an abstract original: the original is one of the STEU items that describe a situation without a named person. Close to a prompt example: the original is one of the five STEU items that the original study's third STEU item-writing prompt shows as examples. Same theme: every other flagged scenario.

Near-verbatim copyClose to a prompt exampleConcrete version of an abstract originalSame themeHuman raters (no categories)

Fourteen of the 42 STEU originals are abstract, describing an appraisal pattern without a named person, such as "A pleasant experience ceases unexpectedly and there is not much that can be done about it." A correct new item for the same emotion has to show the same pattern, so a concrete version of such an item is rated as similar to it. The original study's human raters flagged 2 of ChatGPT-4's STEU scenarios, and each was a concrete version of the abstract original item 24. Each of the five near-verbatim copies reused the scenario of one of the five prompt examples, item 20 four times and item 41 once. Seven more new STEU scenarios reused a prompt example's sentence frame with a different emotion, setting or both, and were not flagged.

A judge that favoured its own items would rate them as less similar to the originals than the other judge does. On the items Claude Opus 5.5 wrote, its highest similarity per scenario averaged 0.27 points below GPT-6.1 Sol's on the 1 to 7 scale. On the eight other models' items, it averaged 0.18 below. The 0.09-point difference is within chance (p = 0.464 before correction). GPT-6.1 Sol showed the opposite pattern: relative to Claude Opus 5.5, it rated its own items 0.09 points more similar to the originals than it rated the other models' items.

Quality of the model-written items

In the original study, 90 to 94 online participants per test took both the original short form and ChatGPT-4's new version, and after each item rated how realistic and how clear it was on a 0 to 100 slider. Instead of testing new people, two model checks were first tested against those participants' data, with pass rules fixed before the first query, and only what passed was applied to the frontier models' new items.

Realism and clarity ratings. Each frontier model, as a rater, rated the realism and clarity of ChatGPT-4's items and the original items 3 times, using the participants' two questions word for word. A rating passed if, with each rater left out in turn, the average of the other nine raters correlated with the participants' average rating more strongly than the median participant's ratings correlated with the other participants' average, with a 95% interval above 0. The table gives these correlations: the median participant's, the lowest of the nine-rater averages, and the ceiling, which is the highest correlation a rater can be expected to reach given how much the participants disagree. Seven of the 19 original items in the STEU short form were reworded for it, and the published data do not include their reworded answer options, so they were left out of this check.

On the STEU, the raters' clarity ratings did not correlate with the participants'. In an analysis that was not planned, they correlated instead with the share of participants who chose the keyed answer (0.57, against −0.06 for the participants' own clarity ratings). On ChatGPT-4's STEU items 7, 8 and 13, whose answer options the original study describes as similar to one another, the raters' mean clarity ratings were 63 to 68 of 100 and the participants' 89 to 91. Over the items used in the check, participants rated ChatGPT-4's STEU items +7.4 points clearer than the originals, and the raters +0.3. The raters may have read clarity as whether one answer is clearly best, and participants as whether the situation is clearly described.

Key check. Each frontier model also took ChatGPT-4's items as a test, 10 times. Together the models form the panel, which disputes an item's key when, counting each model equally, it preferred another option to the key. The check passed if the panel disputed the key of STEU item 18 and of at least 3 of the 5 items that the original study found to perform poorly, and of no more than 3 of the other 14, and if the panel's agreement with the key (the share of its answers that chose the key) correlated with the share of participants who chose it, with a 95% interval above 0. It passed on the STEU. The panel disputed the keys of items 7, 8 and 18 but not of items 4 and 13. On STEU item 18, every panel answer and 90 of the 94 participants chose "compassion" over the keyed "distress". The one other disputed key, on STEU item 10, was not a poorly performing item, but there too the panel chose the participants' most common answer, "shame", over the keyed "distress". The panel's agreement with the key correlated 0.52 (95% interval 0.03 to 0.80) with the share of participants who chose it. ChatGPT-4's STEM items include no poorly performing item, and the panel chose the key in every STEM answer, including on STEM item 18, which 71% of participants answered differently. The STEM key check therefore could not be tested and is not used.

Limitations

  • Both the original six models and the frontier models could have seen the tests in training. The full STEU and STEM items and answer keys have been public since 2008, and the GEMOK-Blends items and key since 2017. The comparison between them is therefore like for like with respect to the test items, but neither set of scores separates understanding from memorisation. The frontier models could also have seen material published after the original six models were tested: the original study, its supplementary material on specific items and its item-level data. Perfect STEU scores fit memorisation but do not prove it, because, as the original study describes it, the STEU key follows from appraisal theory, which a model could apply to unseen items. No memorisation test was run.
  • The human norm samples are small and old. The STEM and STEU norms come from psychology undergraduates in Australia tested for the 2008 publication, and the GEMOK-Blends norm from 180 Mechanical Turk workers. The item-level human data come from the original study's online samples, who took the short forms, with different wording for seven of the 19 STEU short-form items.
  • Procedures differ from the original study. The frontier models received each whole test in one message through the providers' APIs. The original six models received a test split across several messages when it exceeded a character limit. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, whose weights are public, was served by a third-party host (DeepInfra, through the OpenRouter service) at the precision of the released weights. Its scores may differ slightly from those of DeepSeek's own service.
  • Three of the original study's five tests were run. The emotion regulation and emotion management subtests of the Geneva Emotional Competence Test (GECo) were not run, because their items are available only under a signed user agreement with the University of Geneva. GEMOK-Blends was used only for scoring, not for item writing or the paraphrase check.
  • Some choices were made after seeing data. Gemini 3.8 Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash were added after the other eight models had been run and analysed, and GEMOK-Blends after the STEM and STEU results were known. The two judges were chosen after the models had been tested as judges against the human similarity ratings, and the four categories of flagged scenarios were defined after reading the flagged scenarios.
  • No new human data were collected. The model checks of the new items cover realism on both tests, clarity on the STEM and keys on the STEU, but not whether the new tests are reliable or measure the same ability in people as the originals. A panel of current models may also share the frontier models' view of a contested option more than ChatGPT-4's, which would lower the dispute rate for their STEU keys without the keys being better.

Method

How the tests were run, scored and compared

Materials. The STEM and STEU were taken from the APA online supplement to MacCann and Roberts (2008). For the STEU, the keyed answers are the options marked correct there. For the STEM, they are the options most experts chose, with three ties broken by the higher mean expert rating. Both keys matched the original study's scoring files for all 37 short-form items. GEMOK-Blends was taken from the supplement to Schlegel and Scherer (2018), where the keyed pair is underlined, and was given in the original study's item order. Its key matched the original study's scoring file for all 20 items, and scoring the original study's human responses with it reproduced their published mean (0.68).

Runs. Each run was one new conversation with one message: the test's own instructions, every item with lettered options, and a request to answer one line per item. The output limit per response was 128,000 tokens for the Claude models, their maximum, and 393,216 for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, DeepSeek's documented maximum. The OpenAI models were allowed 128,000 tokens on the STEM and STEU and were sent no limit on GEMOK-Blends. Gemini 4 Argon was allowed 32,768 tokens on the STEM and STEU and 1,000,000 on GEMOK-Blends, and Gemini 3.8 Flash 1,000,000 on all tests. No OpenAI or Gemini 4 Argon response came near its limit. A model that reached its limit before answering was asked to continue in the same conversation, up to five times. One continuation was needed in 13 runs, all from Claude Haiku 5.5 or Sonnet 5.5 at maximum effort. Only connection errors before any output were retried. The STEM and STEU runs of the eight models tested first were begun under an earlier protocol, which restarted a run whose response hit the output limit. Responses hit the limit in only three model, effort and test combinations. These were rerun in full, and the earlier runs are excluded. The other 287 runs made under the earlier protocol never reached a limit and were kept.

Comparisons. The effect of effort averaged over the 10 models was tested with an exact sign-flip test. On each of the STEM and STEU, the frontier models' flag count was compared with ChatGPT-4's under the same judges with Fisher's exact test. The pooled realism and clarity ratings were compared with ChatGPT-4's items with Welch's t-test, items as units, and the disputed STEU keys with Fisher's exact test. All tests were two-sided at 0.05 and Holm-corrected within each family of comparisons.

Item writing. Each model's item-writing conversation continued from its first default-effort run of the test. The model was told the correct answers to the items it had missed and then given the original study's item-writing prompts, word for word except that "revise items 58-61" (ChatGPT-4's own numbering) became "revise the 4 reversed-format items". Claude Sonnet 5.5 extracted the items, and every extracted scenario was checked to appear word for word in the model's response. Item writing, similarity judging, the realism and clarity ratings and the panel all used default effort.
